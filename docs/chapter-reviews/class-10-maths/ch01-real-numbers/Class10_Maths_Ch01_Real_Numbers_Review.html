📖 Part 1: Chapter Introduction & Core Concepts
✦ Chapter Introduction
Real numbers constitute the foundation of modern mathematics. Every number that can be plotted on the continuous real number line is a real number. In this chapter, we explore the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, prime factorisation techniques for finding HCF and LCM, and rigorous proofs of irrationality for numbers like √2, √3, and √5.
✦ Standard Definitions
1 Real Numbers (R)
The collection of all rational and irrational numbers together forms the set of real numbers (ℝ). Every real number can be represented by a unique point on the number line.
2 Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.
3 Highest Common Factor (HCF)
The product of the smallest power of each common prime factor involved in the numbers.
4 Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)
The product of the greatest power of each prime factor involved in the numbers.
5 Co-prime Numbers
Two positive integers a and b are said to be co-prime if their highest common factor is 1, i.e., HCF(a, b) = 1.
6 Irrational Number
A number that cannot be written in the form pq (where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0). Its decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating.
✦ Key Conceptual Principles
  • Every composite number can be uniquely factorised as a product of prime powers.
  • For any two positive integers a and b: HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b.
  • If p is a prime number and p divides a², then p divides a (where a is a positive integer).
  • Numbers of the form 4ⁿ and 6ⁿ cannot end with digit 0 because their prime factorisations do not contain the factor 5.
  • The sum, difference, or product of a rational and an irrational number is always irrational.
  • The numbers √2, √3, √5, √p (for any prime p) are irrational numbers.
✦ Master Formulas & Identities
✦ Fundamental Product Identity
HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
✦ LCM Formula
LCM(a, b) = (a × b) / HCF(a, b)

✦ 1. The Real Number System (ℝ)
Real numbers constitute all numbers that can be plotted on the continuous number line. They include both rational and irrational numbers:
• Natural Numbers (ℕ): Counting numbers {1, 2, 3, 4, ...}
• Whole Numbers (W): Counting numbers including zero {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}
• Integers (ℤ): Natural numbers, zero, and negative natural numbers {..., −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...}
• Rational Numbers (ℚ): Any number expressible in the form pq where p, q ∈ ℤ and q ≠ 0.
• Irrational Numbers: Non-terminating, non-repeating decimals that cannot be written in fractional form (e.g., √2, √3, √5, π).
• Hierarchy: ℕ ⊂ W ⊂ ℤ ⊂ ℚ ⊂ ℝ
✦ 2. Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Unique Factorisation)
Theorem Statement: Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.
• Canonical Prime Factorisation: Any composite number x can be written uniquely as:
x = p1a1 × p2a2 × ... × pkak
where p1 < p2 < ... < pk are distinct prime numbers and ai are natural numbers.
• Example: 140 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 7 = 22 × 5 × 7.
• Significance: Proves prime numbers are the indivisible building blocks of all composite integers!
✦ 3. Master HCF & LCM Prime Factorisation Protocol
For any two positive integers a and b expressed in prime factorised form:
Highest Common Factor (HCF):
Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor involved in the numbers.
Example: 6 = 21 × 31, 20 = 22 × 51
⇒ HCF(6, 20) = 21 = 2.
Lowest Common Multiple (LCM):
Product of the greatest power of each prime factor involved in the numbers.
Example: 22 × 31 × 51
⇒ LCM(6, 20) = 4 × 3 × 5 = 60.
✦ Master Two-Number Product Identity:
For any two positive integers a and b:
HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
⚠️ Crucial Note: This identity holds strictly for 2 numbers. For 3 numbers: HCF(p,q,r) × LCM(p,q,r) ≠ p × q × r!
✦ 4. Criteria for Numbers Ending in Zero (10n Rule)
Why do expressions like 4n and 6n never end with the digit 0 for any natural number n?
• Any number ending with 0 must be divisible by 10 (i.e., 2 × 5).
• Thus, its prime factorisation must contain both prime factors 2 and 5.
• For 4n = (22)n = 22n: Prime factor is only 2 (missing 5).
• For 6n = (2 × 3)n = 2n × 3n: Prime factors are only 2 and 3 (missing 5).
• By the uniqueness of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, no other prime factors exist. Hence, neither 4n nor 6n can ever end with digit 0!
✦ 5. Proof by Contradiction (Irrationality Theorem)
Theorem 1.3: Let p be a prime number. If p divides a2 (where a is a positive integer), then p divides a.
The 4-Step Contradiction Method:
Step 1: Assumption: Assume to the contrary that √p is rational ⇒ √p = ab where a, b are co-prime integers (b ≠ 0).
Step 2: Square & Divisibility: p b2 = a2 ⇒ p divides a2 ⇒ p divides a.
Step 3: Substitution: Let a = p c ⇒ p b2 = p2 c2 ⇒ b2 = p c2 ⇒ p divides b.
Step 4: Contradiction: Both a and b share a common factor p, contradicting that a and b are co-prime. Hence, √p is irrational!
✦ 6. Master Revision Formula Cheat Sheet
• Composite Number: Any integer > 1 having more than 2 distinct factors.
• Prime Number: Integer > 1 whose only positive factors are 1 and itself.
• Co-prime Numbers: Two integers a and b are co-prime if HCF(a, b) = 1.
• Two-Number Relation: HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
• Finding LCM from HCF: LCM(a, b) = a × bHCF(a, b)
• Rational ± Irrational: Always Irrational.
• Non-zero Rational × Irrational: Always Irrational.

Check whether 4n can end with the digit 0 for any natural number n.
💡 Solution:
For any number to end with the digit 0, it must be divisible by 10.
This implies that its prime factorisation must contain both the prime numbers 2 and 5.
4n = (22)n = 22n
The only prime factor in the prime factorisation of 4n is 2.
There is no factor 5 in its prime factorisation.
By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, the uniqueness of prime factorisation guarantees that there are no other prime factors in 4n.
So, there is no natural number n for which 4n ends with the digit 0.
✓ Final Answer: Hence, 4n cannot end with the digit 0 for any natural number n.

💡 Solution:

Step 2: To find HCF:
The common prime factor is 2. The smallest power of this common prime factor is 21.

The prime factors involved are 2, 3, and 5.
The greatest powers of these factors are 22, 31, and 51.

✓ Final Answer: HCF(6, 20) = 2,   LCM(6, 20) = 60

Example 5:
Prove that √3 is irrational.
💡 Proof:
Let us assume, to the contrary, that √3 is rational.
Therefore, we can find co-prime integers a and b (where b ≠ 0) such that:
√3 = ab
Squaring both sides:
3 = a2b2  ⇒  3b2 = a2   ...(Equation 1)
Therefore, 3 divides a2.
[By Theorem 1.3: If a prime p divides a2, then p divides a]
So, 3 divides a.
Let a = 3c for some integer c.
Substituting a = 3c in Equation 1:
3b2 = (3c)2 = 9c2
⇒ b2 = 3c2
This implies that 3 divides b2, and therefore 3 divides b.
Thus, a and b have at least 3 as a common factor.
But this contradicts the fact that a and b are co-prime (having no common factor other than 1).
This contradiction arises because of our incorrect assumption that √3 is rational.
✓ Conclusion: Hence, √3 is irrational.

✓ Conclusion: Hence, 5 − √3 is irrational.

💡 Solution:

Taking 13 as a common factor:
= 13 × (7 × 11 + 1)
= 13 × (77 + 1)

Since the given number has factors other than 1 and itself (specifically 13, 78, 6, 2, 3), it is a composite number.

= 5 × (7 × 6 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 + 1)
= 5 × (1008 + 1)
= 5 × 1009
Since the given expression has 5 and 1009 as factors other than 1 and itself, it is a composite number.
✓ Conclusion: Hence, both expressions represent composite numbers.

💡 Proof:
Let us assume, to the contrary, that √5 is rational.
Therefore, we can find co-prime integers a and b (where b ≠ 0) such that:
√5 = ab
Rearranging, we get:
√5 b = a
Squaring both sides:
5b2 = a2   ...(Equation 1)
This implies that 5 divides a2.
By Theorem 1.3, if a prime p divides a2, then p divides a.
Therefore, 5 divides a.
So, we can write a = 5c for some integer c.
Substituting a = 5c in Equation 1:
5b2 = (5c)2
5b2 = 25c2
Dividing both sides by 5:
b2 = 5c2
This means 5 divides b2, and therefore 5 divides b.
From above, both a and b have at least 5 as a common factor.
But this contradicts the fact that a and b are co-prime (having no common factor other than 1).
This contradiction has arisen because of our incorrect assumption that √5 is rational.
✓ Conclusion: Hence, we conclude that √5 is irrational.
