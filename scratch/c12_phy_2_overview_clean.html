📖 Quick Glossary & Basic Definitions

Essential Core Concepts & Key Definitions • Unit II: Current Electricity

1. Electric Current (I): Rate of flow of electric charge across any cross-section: I = dq/dt [Ampere A, Dimensions: [A]]. Scalar quantity.
2. Drift Velocity (vd): Average velocity with which free electrons drift opposite to applied electric field: vd = (e E τ)/m ≈ 10-4 m s-1.
3. Electron Mobility (μ): Magnitude of drift velocity per unit electric field: μ = vd / E = (e τ)/m [m2 V-1 s-1]. Always positive.
4. Resistivity (ρ) & Conductivity (σ): Intrinsic property ρ = m / (n e2 τ) [Ω m], σ = 1/ρ = (n e2 τ)/m [S m-1]. Independent of dimensions.
5. Electromotive Force (EMF, ε) & Internal Resistance (r): Maximum potential difference across open-circuit cell terminals. Terminal voltage during discharge: V = ε - I r.
6. Wheatstone Bridge Condition: When galvanometer current Ig = 0 (null deflection), the ratio of bridge arms satisfies P / Q = R / S.

1. Electric Current, Flow of Electric Charges in Metallic Conductors, Drift Velocity & Mobility

📖 DEFINITION: Drift Velocity (vd)
Average velocity with which free electrons get drifted towards the positive terminal of a conductor under applied electric field: vd = - e Em τ.
SI Unit: m s-1 Dimension: [M0 L1 T-1]
📖 DEFINITION: Electron Mobility (μ)
Magnitude of drift velocity acquired per unit applied electric field: μ = vdE = e τm.
SI Unit: m2 V-1 s-1 Dimension: [M-1 L0 T2 A1]
💡 REAL-WORLD INTUITION: Drift vs Thermal Velocity Analogy
Imagine a huge swarm of bees buzzing wildly at 100 km/h in every random direction (thermal velocity ~105 m/s). When a gentle breeze starts blowing, the entire swarm slowly drifts forward at 1 cm/s (drift velocity ~10-4 m/s). That gentle forward drift is electric current!
⚠️ BOARD EXAM TRAP & CONCEPTUAL PITFALL: Why Light Turns On Instantly Despite Slow Drift Velocity
Free electrons drift at just ~0.1 mm/s. But when you flip a switch, the electric field travels through the wire at nearly the speed of light (~3 × 108 m/s), setting all 1028 electrons per m3 into motion simultaneously across the entire circuit!

Current electricity is the dynamic branch of physics dealing with charges in steady motion. In electrostatics, charges reside at rest on conductors in electrostatic equilibrium where the internal electric field is zero. However, when an external electric field or potential difference is maintained across the ends of a metallic conductor by a battery or external power source, electric charges experience continuous electrostatic forces, establishing a macroscopic continuous electric current.

📌 DEFINITION: Electric Current (I)
Electric current across any cross-sectional area of a conductor is defined as the net rate of flow of electric charge passing through that area per unit time: I = dq / dt. In steady state, I = q / t. SI Unit: Ampere (A) = 1 Coulomb per second (C s-1). Dimensional Formula: [M0 L0 T0 A1]. Electric current is a scalar quantity because it obeys ordinary scalar algebraic laws of addition rather than vector addition laws.

(i) Flow of Charges in Metallic Conductors & Microscopic Electron Dynamics

In solid metallic conductors (such as copper, aluminium, and silver), positive atomic ions are rigidly locked in crystal lattice sites, while valence electrons detach to form an abundant "free electron gas" with enormous number density (n ≈ 8.5 × 1028 free electrons per m3 in copper). In the absence of an applied electric field (thermal equilibrium):

  • Thermal Random Motion: Free electrons move randomly in all possible directions with high thermal speeds (urms = √(3 kB T / m) ≈ 105 to 106 m s-1 at room temperature 300 K).
  • Zero Net Thermal Current: Because velocity vectors are completely random in three dimensions, the average thermal velocity of all electrons across any cross-section is strictly zero: uavg = (1/N) Σ ui = 0. Hence, an isolated metal wire carries zero electric current.

(ii) Drift Velocity (vd) & Relaxation Time (τ)

When a potential difference V is applied across a conductor of length L, a uniform internal electric field E = V / L is established. Each free electron of mass m and charge -e experiences an electrostatic force F = -e E, acquiring an acceleration a = -e E / m directed opposite to E.

📌 DEFINITION: Drift Velocity (vd) & Relaxation Time (τ)
Drift Velocity (vd) is the small, constant average velocity with which free electrons drift slowly through a metallic conductor in the direction opposite to the applied external electric field. Typical magnitude: 10-4 m s-1 (a few tenths of a millimeter per second).

Relaxation Time (τ) is the average time interval elapsed between two successive collisions of a drifting free electron with the heavy positive metal lattice ions. Typical value for copper at room temperature: τ ≈ 10-14 s.
📐 STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION: Drift Velocity Formula
1. Acceleration of an Electron:
Under electric field E, acceleration a = F / m = (-e E) / m.

2. Velocity of the i-th Electron:
If the i-th electron had initial random thermal velocity ui just after its last collision and time elapsed since that collision is ti:
vi = ui + a ti = ui - e Em ti.

3. Average Velocity (Drift Velocity vd):
vd = 1N Σ vi = 1N Σ ui - e Em [1N Σ ti].

Since 1N Σ ui = 0 (zero net thermal velocity) and 1N Σ ti = τ (average relaxation time):

vd = - e E τm  |  Magnitude: vd = e E τm = e V τm L

(iii) Derivation of Relation Between Electric Current and Drift Velocity

📐 STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION: Relation Between Electric Current & Drift Velocity (I = n e A vd)
1. Geometry: Consider a metallic conductor of length L, uniform cross-sectional area A, and free electron number density n (electrons per m3).

2. Total Number of Free Electrons in Conductor:
N = n × Volume = n × (A × L).

3. Total Mobile Charge in Conductor:
q = N × e = (n A L) e.

4. Transit Time across Conductor:
Time taken by an electron to drift through entire length L with drift speed vd is t = L / vd.

5. Resulting Electric Current:
I = qt = n A L eL / vd = n e A vd.

I = n e A vd  |  Current Density: j = IA = n e vd

(iv) Electron Mobility (μ)

📌 DEFINITION: Mobility (μ)
Mobility of a charge carrier is defined as the magnitude of its drift velocity per unit electric field applied: μ = vd / E = (e τ) / m. SI Unit: m2 V-1 s-1 (or m2 N-1 C s-1). Dimensional Formula: [M-1 L0 T2 A1]. Mobility is always a positive scalar physical quantity. Current in terms of mobility: I = n e A μ E.
⚠️ BOARD EXAM FOCUS & COMMON TRAPS: Why do Electric Bulbs Glow Instantly if Drift Speed is so Tiny (~10-4 m s-1)?
Although individual free electrons drift at a tiny speed of ≈ 0.1 mm s-1, closing the switch establishes an electromagnetic wave/electric field throughout the entire closed circuit almost instantaneously at the speed of light in vacuum (c ≈ 3 × 108 m s-1). Free electrons present throughout all points of the conductor begin drifting simultaneously at the very same instant, lighting the bulb immediately without having to wait for electrons to travel from the switch.
📝 SOLVED BOARD EXAMPLE 1:
A copper wire of cross-sectional area 1.0 × 10-7 m2 carries a steady current of 1.5 A. Assuming free electron density of copper is 8.5 × 1028 m-3, calculate: (a) Current density in the wire, (b) Drift velocity of free electrons. (Take e = 1.6 × 10-19 C).
Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Current Density (j):
j = I / A = (1.5 A) / (1.0 × 10-7 m2) = 1.5 × 107 A m-2.

(b) Drift Velocity (vd):
Using I = n e A vd → vd = I / (n e A) = j / (n e):
vd = (1.5 × 107) / [(8.5 × 1028) × (1.6 × 10-19)]
vd = (1.5 × 107) / (1.36 × 1010) = 1.10 × 10-3 m s-1 = 1.10 mm s-1.

2. Ohm's Law, Electrical Resistivity, Conductivity & Temperature Dependence of Resistance

📖 DEFINITION: Electrical Resistivity (ρ)
Intrinsic resistance offered by unit length of conductor with unit cross-sectional area: ρ = mn e2 τ  |  Conductivity: σ = 1 / ρ = n e2 τm.
SI Unit: Ohm-meter (Ω m) Dimension: [M1 L3 T-3 A-2]
💡 REAL-WORLD INTUITION: Resistance vs Resistivity
Resistivity (ρ) is an intrinsic material property like the stickiness of honey or mud. Resistance (R) is the total difficulty of moving through a specific pipe: R = ρ L / A (longer pipe = more resistance; wider pipe = less resistance).
📝 WORKED BOARD NUMERICAL #1
Problem: A copper wire has 8.5 × 1028 free electrons/m3, length 3.0 m, and cross-section 2.0 × 10-6 m2 carrying 3.0 A. Find the drift velocity of electrons.
Solution:
Formula: vd = I / (n e A)
vd = 3.0 / [(8.5 × 1028) × (1.6 × 10-19) × (2.0 × 10-6)] = 3.0 / [2.72 × 104] = 1.10 × 10-4 m/s (0.11 mm/s).

In 1827, German physicist Georg Simon Ohm experimentally discovered the fundamental linear relationship governing the flow of electric current through metallic conductors.

📌 DEFINITION: Ohm's Law & Electrical Resistance (R)
Ohm's Law: Under constant physical conditions (such as temperature, mechanical strain, and pressure), the electric current (I) flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference (V) applied across its terminals: V ∝ I ⇒ V = I R.

Electrical Resistance (R): The opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current through it: R = V / I. SI Unit: Ohm (Ω) = 1 Volt / 1 Ampere (V A-1). Dimensional Formula: [M1 L2 T-3 A-2].

(i) Microscopic Deduction of Ohm's Law from Drift Theory

📐 STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION: Deduction of Ohm's Law (R = ρ L / A)
1. Drift Velocity Expression:
vd = e E τm = e V τm L   (since electric field E = V / L).

2. Substitute vd into Current Equation:
I = n e A vd = n e A [e V τm L] = [n e2 τ Am L] V.

3. Rearranging for Potential Difference V:
V = [mn e2 τ LA] I = R I.

At constant temperature, m, n, e, τ, L, and A are strictly constant, so V / I = Constant = R.

4. Electrical Resistivity (ρ):
R = ρ LA  ⇒  ρ = mn e2 τ.

5. Electrical Conductivity (σ):
σ = 1ρ = n e2 τm. Vector form of Ohm's Law: j = σ E.

(ii) Temperature Dependence of Electrical Resistance & Resistivity

The electrical resistivity ρ = m / (n e2 τ) depends on free electron density n and relaxation time τ. When temperature T rises:

  • Metals / Metallic Conductors: Electron density n is constant. Increased thermal vibrations of lattice ions increase the frequency of collisions, causing relaxation time τ to decrease. Hence, resistivity ρ increases with temperature.
    RT = R0 [1 + α (T - T0)]  |  ρT = ρ0 [1 + α (T - T0)]
    Here α is the positive temperature coefficient of resistance (for Cu, α ≈ +0.0039 K-1).
  • Semiconductors & Insulators: Thermal energy breaks covalent bonds, causing free carrier density n to increase exponentially [n ∝ e-Eg/(2 kB T)]. The massive increase in n dominates over the slight decrease in τ, causing resistivity to decrease exponentially with temperature (α is negative).
  • Standard Resistance Alloys (Manganin, Constantan, Nichrome): High resistivity with nearly zero temperature coefficient (α ≈ 10-5 K-1). Therefore, they are universally used to construct standard resistance boxes and potentiometer wires.

3. V-I Characteristics: Linear (Ohmic) & Non-Linear (Non-Ohmic) Devices

Electrical devices and materials are broadly categorized based on their current-voltage (V-I) response curves:

  • Ohmic Conductors: Conductors that obey Ohm's law strictly across all voltages. Their V-I graph is a perfectly straight line passing through the origin (e.g. Copper, Silver wire at constant temperature). Resistance R = V / I = 1 / (slope of I-V graph).
  • Non-Ohmic Conductors / Devices: Devices that violate Ohm's law. Their V-I graph is non-linear, does not pass through origin, or is asymmetrical under polarity reversal. Key examples:
    1. P-N Junction Diode: High non-linear current in forward bias (exponential), negligible current in reverse bias until breakdown.
    2. Thyristor: Exhibits multiple conduction states and switching regions.
    3. Gallium Arsenide (GaAs): Displays a Negative Differential Resistance region where current decreases as voltage increases (dI/dV < 0).
    4. Electrolytes & Vacuum Tubes: Non-linear due to space charge and ionic back EMF.

4. Electrical Energy, Electrical Power & Joule's Heating Effect

📌 DEFINITION: Electrical Energy & Electrical Power (P)
Electrical Energy (W): The total work done by a source of EMF in maintaining a steady current I through an electric circuit of resistance R for time t: W = V I t = I2 R t = (V2 / R) t [Joules J].

Electrical Power (P): The rate at which electrical energy is consumed or dissipated into heat: P = dW / dt = V I = I2 R = V2 / R. SI Unit: Watt (W) = 1 Joule per second (J s-1). Commercial unit: 1 Kilowatt-hour (1 kWh) = 1 Board of Trade Unit = 3.6 × 106 J.

(i) Power Dissipation in Series vs Parallel Bulb Combinations

  • Resistance from Rated Values: For an electric bulb rated as (Prated, Vrated), its filament resistance is R = Vrated2 / Prated (Higher rated power ⇒ Lower resistance).
  • Bulbs Connected in Series: Current I is identical through all bulbs. Power dissipated P = I2 R ∝ R. Therefore, the bulb with higher resistance (lower rated power) glows brighter.
  • Bulbs Connected in Parallel: Voltage V is identical across all bulbs. Power dissipated P = V2 / R ∝ 1/R. Therefore, the bulb with lower resistance (higher rated power) glows brighter.

5. Internal Resistance of a Cell, Potential Difference & Electromotive Force (EMF)

📖 DEFINITION: Electromotive Force (EMF) & Internal Resistance (Ε, r)
Maximum potential difference between the terminals of a cell in an open circuit (when no current is drawn): Ε = W / q. Internal resistance: r = (ΕV - 1) R.
SI Unit: Volt (V), Ohm (Ω) Dimension: [M1 L2 T-3 A-1]
💡 REAL-WORLD INTUITION: EMF vs Terminal Voltage Analogy
EMF (Ε) is the gross salary promised by an employer ($100). The cell's internal resistance is like an unavoidable commuting tax (Ir = $10). Terminal voltage (V = Ε - Ir = $90) is the net take-home cash available to spend on external circuit appliances (R)!
⚠️ BOARD EXAM TRAP & CONCEPTUAL PITFALL: Terminal Voltage Exceeding EMF During Cell Charging
During normal discharging: V = Ε - I r (V < Ε). But when a secondary cell is being CHARGED by an external charger, current flows into the positive terminal: V = Ε + I r (Terminal voltage V is strictly GREATER than EMF Ε!).

An electrochemical cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy through redox reactions occurring at its electrodes. Within the cell, non-electrostatic chemical forces perform work to pump positive charge from the lower potential cathode to the higher potential anode against electrostatic repulsive fields.

📌 DEFINITION: EMF (ε), Terminal Potential Difference (V) & Internal Resistance (r)
Electromotive Force (EMF, ε): The maximum potential difference between the electrodes of a cell when no current is drawn from it (open circuit): ε = dW / dq [Volt V, Dimensions: [M L2 T-3 A-1]].

Terminal Potential Difference (V): The potential difference between the terminals of a cell when current I is drawn in a closed circuit.

Internal Resistance (r): The opposition offered by the chemical electrolyte and electrodes of the cell to the flow of internal ionic current. Formula: r = R [(ε / V) - 1].

(i) Mathematical Relationship During Different Operational Modes

  • Discharging Mode (Cell supplying current to load R):
    I = ε / (R + r)  ⇒  V = ε - I r   (Terminal voltage V < ε)
  • Charging Mode (External charger forcing current into cell from + to -):
    V = ε + I r   (Terminal voltage V > ε)
  • Open Circuit Condition (I = 0):
    V = ε   (Terminal voltage equals true EMF)
  • Short Circuit Condition (R = 0):
    Imax = ε / r   (Terminal voltage V = 0, maximum dangerous current)

6. Kirchhoff's Rules & Wheatstone Bridge Principle

📖 DEFINITION: Kirchhoff's Rules (Σ I = 0, Σ ΔV = 0)
1. Junction Rule (KCL): Σ I = 0 (Conservation of Charge).
2. Loop Rule (KVL): Σ ΔV = Σ Ε - Σ I R = 0 (Conservation of Energy).
SI Unit: Ampere (A), Volt (V) Dimension: [A1], [M1 L2 T-3 A-1]
💡 REAL-WORLD INTUITION: Wheatstone Bridge Balance Analogy
Think of a Wheatstone bridge as a balanced two-pan weighing scale. When the ratio of left arms equals the ratio of right arms (P / Q = R / S), both bridge nodes reach the EXACT SAME electric potential (VB = VD). Zero current flows through the central galvanometer branch (Ig = 0)!

While simple series-parallel circuits can be reduced using Ohm's law, complex electrical networks containing multiple loops, branches, and interconnected electromotive sources require Kirchhoff's Laws, formulated by Gustav Kirchhoff in 1845.

📌 DEFINITION: Kirchhoff's First Rule (Junction Rule / KCL)
Kirchhoff's Junction Rule states that the algebraic sum of electric currents entering and leaving any electrical node or junction in a circuit is strictly zero: Σ I = 0. Sum of entering currents = Sum of leaving currents. This rule is a direct physical consequence of the Law of Conservation of Electric Charge.
📌 DEFINITION: Kirchhoff's Second Rule (Loop Rule / KVL)
Kirchhoff's Loop Rule states that the algebraic sum of changes in electrostatic potential around any closed mesh or loop of an electrical circuit is strictly zero: Σ ΔV = 0 ⇒ Σ ε = Σ I R. This rule is a direct physical consequence of the Law of Conservation of Energy (electrostatic field is conservative).

(i) Wheatstone Bridge Network & Balanced Condition Derivation

📐 STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION: Balanced Wheatstone Bridge Condition (P / Q = R / S)
1. Circuit Setup:
Four resistors P, Q, R, S form four arms of a quadrilateral ABCD. Battery of EMF ε is connected across opposite junctions A and C; Galvanometer G (resistance G) is connected across junctions B and D.

2. Applying Kirchhoff's Junction Rule:
At node B: I1 = Ig + IQ  ⇒  IQ = I1 - Ig.
At node D: I2 + Ig = IS  ⇒  IS = I2 + Ig.

3. Applying Kirchhoff's Loop Rule to Mesh ABDA:
- I1 P - Ig G + I2 R = 0  ⇒  I1 P + Ig G = I2 R  --- (Equation 1).

4. Applying Kirchhoff's Loop Rule to Mesh BCDB:
- (I1 - Ig) Q + (I2 + Ig) S + Ig G = 0  --- (Equation 2).

5. Balanced Bridge Condition (Null Deflection Ig = 0):
When potential at B equals potential at D (VB = VD), zero current flows through galvanometer (Ig = 0).
From Equation (1): I1 P = I2 R.
From Equation (2): I1 Q = I2 S.

6. Dividing the two equations:
I1 PI1 Q = I2 RI2 S  ⇒  PQ = RS

Conclusion: Under balanced condition, ratio P/Q equals R/S. The galvanometer can be interchanged with the battery without disturbing the null condition.

7. Master Comparison Matrices

Parameter Electromotive Force (EMF, ε) Terminal Potential Difference (V)
1. Definition Max work done by cell in driving unit charge through entire complete circuit. Work done in moving unit charge between two specific circuit points.
2. Circuit State Measured when circuit is open (no current I = 0). Measured when circuit is closed (current I > 0).
3. Dependence on R Independent of external load resistance R. Directly depends on external load resistance R.
4. Cause vs Effect EMF is the cause of current flow. Potential difference is the effect of current flow.
5. Magnitude Always greater than V during discharge (ε > V). V = ε - I r (less than ε during discharge).
